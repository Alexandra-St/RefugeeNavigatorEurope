# Working in Denmark: work authorization and employee rights

**Summary**

An overview of work authorization under Denmark’s Ukraine Special Act and practical points to check in an employment contract, at work and with the Danish authorities. The right to work depends on your current residence basis and personal situation.

**Content**

## Who can work in Denmark

If you have been granted a residence permit under the Ukraine Special Act, the Danish Immigration Service says you are entitled to work while that permit is valid. Section 1 and section 2 have different eligibility conditions. Check the decision and your current status; having an application, CPR number or tax card is not the same as having a residence permit.

If you are waiting for a decision, the Immigration Service says you may work after it has recorded your fingerprints and facial image. This does not apply if you obviously cannot qualify under section 1 or 2. If you or a potential employer is unsure whether this exception applies, the authority says you should not start work before receiving a decision. An application by itself is not an unconditional right to work.

Employers must check a foreign employee’s right to work. Show your actual residence decision or card and ask the Immigration Service or SIRI if your work permission or status is unclear. Do not assume that an application, a job offer or a document from another country gives permission to work in Denmark.

## Employment contracts

The written-information rules generally cover employees whose predetermined or actual working time averages more than 3 hours a week over 4 consecutive weeks. Hours worked for employers in the same company, group or entity are combined. The rules also cover employment where no guaranteed amount of paid hours was set before work began. Seafarers are covered by separate legislation. A qualifying collective agreement may replace some statutory requirements if it provides equivalent protection or meets the law’s specific conditions. Whether an exception applies depends on the agreement and the law.

Where these rules apply, essential employment terms must be provided in writing. Some specified information is due within 7 calendar days after employment starts; other information is due within one month. These are information-delivery deadlines, not a minimum job duration or the threshold for legal coverage.

Depending on the job, written information can cover the employer and employee, the job and start date, pay and payment frequency, working hours and overtime arrangements, holiday arrangements, notice terms, any probation period, and applicable collective agreements. The exact terms depend on the work and the rules that apply to it.

Read what you receive and keep a copy. There is no single probation or notice period for every worker. If terms are unclear, ask your employer or union.

## Salary and working hours

Denmark has no statutory nationwide minimum wage. Pay is usually set by a collective agreement or negotiated individually. The employer must pay the wage agreed for your work, but the amount and supplements vary by job and agreement.

Around 37 hours a week is common in many full-time jobs, but it is not a universal statutory working week. Check your agreed hours, breaks, rest periods and how overtime, evening, night or weekend work is handled. Legal working-time and rest rules apply, with exceptions in some situations. There is no universal overtime rate or guaranteed supplement.

Keep your contract, payslips and any record of hours worked. Compare the pay and deductions with your agreed terms and ask your employer or SKAT about anything you do not understand.

## Holidays, sickness and working conditions

The general statutory holiday entitlement is five weeks, but how holiday pay is provided and how much you have accrued depend on your employment and pay arrangement. Check your contract and holiday information rather than assuming a particular payment.

Sick-pay rules also differ. Salaried employees covered by the Salaried Employees Act generally receive full pay during sickness; other employees may have pay under a contract or collective agreement, or may need to meet separate sickness-benefit conditions. Tell your employer about illness as required by your workplace rules and check the relevant terms.

## Taxes and registration

If you start a job, contact the Danish Tax Agency (SKAT) about a tax card and the personal tax-number process. The documents and registration route depend on your situation. If you do not yet have a CPR number, do not assume that the tax process is impossible or that the same steps apply to every person; ask SKAT what applies to you.

Keep your expected income and relevant deductions updated in your preliminary income assessment (forskudsopgørelse) so monthly tax withholding can reflect your circumstances. Which deductions apply depends on your situation.

A CPR number, a personal tax number where relevant, and a tax card are used for registration or tax purposes; none is a residence permit or gives work permission by itself. If you are waiting for a Special Act decision, follow the Immigration Service’s instructions and confirm the tax-card route with SKAT.

## Workplace rights and where to raise a concern

Employers must provide a safe and healthy working environment, including assessing risks and giving workers appropriate instruction. Workers also have responsibilities to follow safety instructions and raise problems. The Danish Working Environment Authority, Arbejdstilsynet, handles work-environment and safety concerns. It accepts anonymous complaints about suspected violations and certain abusive behaviour.

If you experience discrimination at work, Danish equality laws may apply. Depending on the facts, a complaint may be made to the Board on Equal Treatment or you may seek help through a trade union or the courts. The route and possible outcome depend on the case.

If you are injured at work, tell your employer promptly and keep relevant records. Employers or doctors report many work injuries. Under applicable conditions, an injured worker can also report the injury directly. The Labour Market Insurance (AES) must recognize an injury before compensation can be paid. Reporting does not guarantee compensation.

## If a job ends or you change employers

Check the notice and final-pay terms in your contract or collective agreement. If you are registered with a municipality, an a-kasse or a benefit scheme, follow the instructions that apply to your own registration; Jobnet or Joblog requirements do not apply to everyone in the same way.

Unemployment insurance in Denmark is voluntary. You are not automatically a member of an a-kasse because you work or pay tax. Membership requires an application and contributions, and unemployment benefits have additional conditions. Membership of a trade union is separate and also voluntary. Municipal financial assistance is a different system and has separate rules.

The Special Act pages confirm work rights for permit holders and set out a conditional rule for some pending applicants. They do not answer every question about a change of job, an expired or revoked permit, an appeal, or a change of residence basis. If your status changes or your document lists a work restriction, ask the Immigration Service or SIRI before relying on continued work permission.

## Where to get help

For residence and work permission, contact the Danish Immigration Service or SIRI and refer to your own decision and documents. For tax cards and personal tax numbers, contact SKAT. For workplace safety, contact Arbejdstilsynet. A trade union can advise its members on pay and employment conditions; membership is voluntary. An a-kasse handles its members’ unemployment-insurance questions. Ask the relevant authority or organization which language assistance is available.

This overview is general information, not an assessment of an individual case. Rules and official guidance can change. Check current sources before acting.
